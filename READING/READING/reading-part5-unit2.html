The homes of the future viewed from today

Mark Finchley reviews TV series Tomorrow's Homes

Having just watched the whole of Channel 8's TV series Tomorrow's Homes, I've been wondering about how anyone can predict the future of domestic life. You'd imagine that if you knew what architects and technology companies were developing now to make life easier, more exciting and more beautiful, you'd have a pretty good idea of what to expect in tomorrow's homes. In reality, it's more complicated, and just as much about what we'll choose to hang on to from today's — the things that are 'future-proof'. In the 1950s, people thought that in the twenty-first century household tasks would be done by labour-saving devices or robots — with food pills for dinner. Yet people still wash up and cook, even though the technology exists that makes neither of these tasks necessary.

Exam task: Read the text and choose the correct answer (A, B, C or D).

1. The writer makes the point in the first paragraph that predicting how homes will be in the future...

Answer: B · Evidence from the text “it's more complicated, and just as much about what we'll choose to hang on to from today's — the things that are 'future-proof'” The writer says prediction isn't just about new developments (what architects/tech companies are building) — it's just as much about what stays the same, so B captures this better than C.

2. What does 'today's' refer to in the first paragraph?

Answer: C · Evidence from the text “just as much about what we'll choose to hang on to from today's — the things that are 'future-proof'” ‘Today's’ is short for ‘today's homes’ — the reference word points back to ‘tomorrow's homes’ mentioned just before, contrasted with our current ones.

Tomorrow's Homes, however, dared to make predictions which it turned into reality using an average home belonging to a family called the Forseys. Four miles of cable were installed in the house so that all the electrics, from lights to the fridge, could be controlled via the internet, and various other devices and gadgets were introduced in addition to this. The family were then filmed as they got used to their new home life. Programme presenter Harry Thwaites is also a consultant who spends his work life imagining the future, so testing out his ideas for the programme was a fascinating experiment for him. His approach was to use technology that was not totally brand new, but had only recently become more affordable. CCTV cameras for security have been around for years, for example, but they are no longer only an option for the mega-rich.

3. According to the second paragraph, the technology installed in the Forseys' house...

Answer: B · Evidence from the text “CCTV cameras for security have been around for years, for example, but they are no longer only an option for the mega-rich.” The technology wasn't brand new (ruling out A and D) — the point is that it used to be limited to rich people and has now become more widely affordable.

The Forsey family consists of a husband and wife with four children and two grandchildren. They appear to be very natural and ordinary on the programme, and it was always interesting to see how they reacted to the technology they were testing. One example that sticks in the mind is when Janine, the mother, enters her reconstructed, all-white home (after successfully unlocking her new front door by using her thumb print as a key), and she immediately bursts into tears — quite understandably it has to be said. A short while later, her husband Ben gets locked out because the skin on his thumb is too rough. As the series progresses, however, they slowly come to accept the technology, and even start to believe it could have some value in their lives.

4. What does 'sticks' mean in the third paragraph?

Answer: B · Evidence from the text “One example that sticks in the mind is when Janine…” ‘Sticks in the mind’ is a fixed expression meaning something is memorable — it stays/remains in your memory.

5. According to the third paragraph, how did the family members react to the new technology?

Answer: A · Evidence from the text “As the series progresses, however, they slowly come to accept the technology, and even start to believe it could have some value in their lives.” The paragraph traces a journey from initial frustration (tears, being locked out) to gradual acceptance — a positive shift over time.

I was keen to see during the show if anything emerged as potentially future-proof, and there were some great examples. To help Janine deal with various worries, she was provided with a mind-controlled relaxation tool. This was a kind of headband connected to a DVD, which, incredibly, she could control with her thoughts. When she relaxed mentally, she made an image of the sun go down, as it would at night, on the DVD. When she had tried the gadget and achieved the sun set, she was asked how effective the gadget had been. Janine commented, 'Nothing can compare to a nice cup of tea and a good soap opera!'

6. How did Janine feel about the mind-controlled relaxation tool?

Answer: D · Evidence from the text “Nothing can compare to a nice cup of tea and a good soap opera!” Despite the gadget “working” (she achieved the sunset), her comment shows she still prefers old-fashioned relaxation — tea and TV — over the high-tech tool.

Exercise 2

Complete the text with the correct alternatives.

What will our homes be like in 2030?

There are some things that we cannot predict about the world in 2030, but others seem certain. For example, we are definitely (1) (1) ______ many more homes in larger cities as the world’s population (2)

Answer: going to have ‘Be going to’ is used for a prediction based on present evidence — population growth is already a known, observable trend, so going to have fits, not the -ing form alone.

(2) ______ to grow. But what will these homes be like?

Answer: continues After as (meaning 'because'), the present simple is used for a general, ongoing fact — continues is correct here, not a future form.

Firstly, ‘The Internet of Things’ (3) (3) ______ an established part of everyone’s lives by then.

Answer: will be Will be is a simple future prediction. Will have been (future perfect) would need a sense of 'completed before a point', which doesn't fit as naturally here.

All electronic devices in your home will be connected, and they (4) (4) ______ with each other constantly.

Answer: will be communicating The future continuous (will be communicating) describes an ongoing action happening at a point in the future — not the present simple.

By 2030, you also (5) (5) ______ your house with smart technology, so you’ll have movement, temperature and humidity sensors throughout the house.

Answer: will have equipped The future perfect (will have equipped) is used for an action completed before a specific future point — signalled by ‘By 2030’.

These (6) (6) ______ the environment in your house constantly.

Answer: will be measuring The future continuous (will be measuring) fits a repeated, ongoing action at a future time — sensors constantly measuring, not a completed action.

In fact, they (7) (7) ______ to tell you if you’ve left a door open, or a tap on!

Answer: will even be able Will be able to expresses future ability — the whole passage is about 2030, a future point, so can (present ability) doesn't fit.
