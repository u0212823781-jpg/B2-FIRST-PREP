Part 5 — Reading (multiple choice)

Taking the plunge

By Priya Nandan

When my flatmate suggested we spend our week off doing a diving course in Cyprus, my first reaction was pure panic. I have never been what you'd call a strong swimmer, and the idea of breathing through a tube several metres under the surface of the sea struck me as somewhere between reckless and impossible. She, on the other hand, had been diving twice before and talked about it the way some people talk about their favourite restaurant — with a kind of dreamy enthusiasm that made no sense to me at all. In the end, more out of not wanting to spend the week alone in the hotel than any real desire to dive, I signed up.

Part 5 — Exam task: Read the text and choose the correct answer (A, B, C or D).

1. What does the writer suggest about her flatmate's invitation?

Answer: B The writer is explicit that her real motive wasn't wanting to dive, but not wanting to be alone — ruling out C, and there's no mention of pressure (D) or long-term planning (A).

The first two days were spent almost entirely in a swimming pool, which surprised me — I had pictured us in the open sea by lunchtime on day one. Our instructor, a patient Dutchman named Bram, seemed entirely unbothered by how long it took me to feel comfortable simply breathing underwater through the regulator. Looking back, I'm grateful he didn't rush things; several of the skills we practised in the pool — clearing water from a mask, sharing air with a diving partner — later turned out to matter far more than I expected. At the time, though, I mostly just wanted to get to the 'real' diving.

2. How does the writer feel, looking back, about the time spent in the pool?

Answer: C ‘Grateful’ now contrasts directly with ‘At the time, though, I mostly just wanted to get to the real diving’ — a shift in attitude with hindsight, not a lasting complaint.

Once we finally did descend into open water on the third day, I understood immediately what my flatmate had been trying, and failing, to describe. There is a particular kind of quiet down there that has nothing in common with silence on land; you can hear yourself breathe, and very little else. What I hadn't anticipated was how physically demanding it would be — by the end of each dive, my legs ached from the unfamiliar effort of finning, and I was always the first back on the boat, exhausted. Still, I never once considered giving up, which, given how close I'd come to cancelling the whole trip, surprised me more than anyone.

3. What did the writer find most surprising about the open-water dive?

Answer: B ‘What I hadn't anticipated’ signals the surprise, and it's specifically about physical tiredness — not fear, which isn't mentioned in this paragraph at all.

4. What does the writer say about her attitude towards finishing the course?

Answer: B Despite the exhaustion just described, she says she ‘never once considered giving up’ — clear determination, with no mention of her flatmate as the reason.

On our last dive, Bram led us to a small reef where, without any warning, an enormous sea turtle appeared out of the blue and swam alongside us for what felt like several minutes before losing interest and drifting away. I have watched countless nature documentaries in my life, and none of them prepared me for how it would feel to be, briefly, just another creature in someone else's territory. My flatmate, treading water beside me, gave me a look through her mask that needed no translation.

5. How did the writer feel about seeing the turtle?

Answer: C This describes a new, unexpected feeling of being a visitor in the turtle's world — documentaries are mentioned only to say they didn't prepare her for this feeling, so D is a distortion.

Since we got home, I've noticed how differently I now talk about the sea, and about trying new things generally. I used to assume that being bad at something on day one meant I probably shouldn't bother continuing, but this trip taught me that discomfort in the early stages doesn't predict very much at all. I've already booked an advanced course for next year, and I suspect it won't be the last.

6. What has the writer concluded from the whole experience?

Answer: B This is the writer's stated lesson, directly contradicting her old assumption that struggling on day one meant she ‘probably shouldn't bother continuing’.

Part 6 — Reading (gapped text)

The secret life of city foxes

Part 6 — Exam task: Six sentences have been removed from the text. Choose from sentences A–G the one which fits each gap (1–6). There is one extra sentence you do not need to use.

Anyone who has lived in a British city for more than a year or two has probably spotted a fox trotting along a garden wall at dusk, entirely unbothered by the nearby traffic. What used to be a rare and slightly thrilling sighting has become, for many urban residents, a fairly ordinary part of daily life. So how did a woodland animal end up thriving in some of the country's busiest cities?

The shift began gradually, in the years following the Second World War, as British cities expanded outward and new suburbs, with their gardens and hedgerows, crept into what had once been open countryside. [GAP 1] Rather than retreating further from the advancing houses, foxes discovered that gardens offered many of the same advantages as their original woodland habitat, with rather less competition for food.

Gap 1. Which sentence best completes this gap?

Answer: D The missing sentence has to bridge the expanding suburbs just mentioned with the foxes' reaction described right after — option D describes foxes moving in ‘instead of being pushed out’, which matches both sides.

Today, food is rarely the challenge it once was for urban foxes. [GAP 2] Overflowing bins, deliberately left-out scraps and even pet food dishes left on back doorsteps all provide a steady, low-effort source of calories that a fox would struggle to match hunting rabbits in the countryside.

Gap 2. Which sentence best completes this gap?

Answer: G The sentence after the gap lists specific easy food sources — option G's ‘abundance of easy meals for an animal willing to be opportunistic’ introduces exactly that idea before the examples are given.

This reliable food supply has had a measurable effect on fox behaviour. [GAP 3] Urban foxes, several studies have found, tend to have smaller territories than their rural relatives, since a small patch of city street can provide as much food as a much larger stretch of open countryside.

Gap 3. Which sentence best completes this gap?

Answer: A Option A's ‘changed how much ground an individual fox needs to cover’ leads naturally into the next sentence about smaller territories — it names the specific effect the paragraph then explains.

Not everyone welcomes their new neighbours, however. [GAP 4] Complaints about foxes digging up flowerbeds, scattering rubbish or waking residents with their distinctive night-time calls are common, and a small number of people remain convinced, despite little evidence, that foxes pose a serious danger to pets or children.

Gap 4. Which sentence best completes this gap?

Answer: F Option F mentions councils receiving complaints — a direct, logical link to the specific complaints (digging, rubbish, noise) listed in the sentence that follows.

Researchers who study urban foxes tend to push back against this reputation. [GAP 5] Attacks on humans are exceptionally rare, and the vast majority of interactions between foxes and pets amount to nothing more than a brief standoff before the fox moves on.

Gap 5. Which sentence best completes this gap?

Answer: C Option C explains researchers' reasoning (foxes are naturally cautious), which the following sentence then supports with evidence (rare attacks, brief standoffs) — a clear cause-then-evidence link.

Whatever Londoners, Bristolians or Mancunians make of their vulpine neighbours, one thing seems certain: foxes are not going anywhere. [GAP 6] For better or worse, the fox has become as much a feature of the modern British city as the pigeon or the parked car.

Gap 6. Which sentence best completes this gap?

Answer: E Option E's point about fox-friendly habitat increasing as cities grow supports and extends the claim that foxes ‘are not going anywhere’, setting up the final sentence's comparison to pigeons and parked cars.

Part 7 — Reading (multiple matching)

Four people talk about their first job

A — Marcus

My first proper job was in a call centre selling insurance, which I took purely because it paid better than anything else advertised in the local paper that summer. I hated almost every minute of it — the script we had to follow felt completely unnatural, and I was terrible at persuading people to buy things they clearly didn't want. What I didn't expect was how much I'd learn about handling rejection calmly; after a few months of being hung up on multiple times a day, very little fazes me anymore. I lasted exactly one year before moving into something I actually enjoyed.

B — Elena

I started working in my aunt's bakery at fifteen, mostly because she needed the help and I needed pocket money. The hours before school were brutal, and I remember being permanently covered in flour that never quite washed out of my school jumper. Even so, I loved the precision of it — weighing, timing, watching dough change under your hands — and I think that job is the reason I ended up training as a pastry chef years later. My aunt still jokes that she trained me before I even knew it.

C — Tomasz

Technically my first job was mowing neighbours' lawns as a kid, but the first one that paid a proper wage was stacking shelves at a supermarket the summer I turned sixteen. It was mind-numbingly repetitive, and my manager at the time seemed to take pleasure in finding fault with how I'd arranged the tinned tomatoes. What kept me going was the team of other teenagers on the same shift — we turned even the dullest tasks into a kind of competition, and some of those colleagues are still close friends now, over a decade later.

D — Freya

I was hired as a receptionist at a small law firm straight after finishing my exams, largely because I could type fast and sound polite on the phone. The work itself was fairly dull, but I was constantly around solicitors discussing real cases, and that's really what hooked me — I applied to study law the following year almost entirely because of what I'd overheard in that office. Looking back, I doubt I would have chosen that career path without having taken a job that, on paper, had nothing to do with it.

Part 7 — Exam task: Which person...

1. Which person was unhappy with how a manager treated them?

Answer: C — Tomasz Tomasz's manager ‘seemed to take pleasure in finding fault’ with his work — a clear negative comment about the manager's attitude.

2. Which person took the job mainly for financial reasons?

Answer: A — Marcus Marcus says explicitly he took the job because it ‘paid better’, with no other reason given.

3. Which person says the experience made them better at staying calm under pressure?

Answer: A — Marcus Marcus describes learning to handle rejection calmly and no longer being easily bothered — a direct skill gained from the job.

4. Which person was inspired to pursue a completely different career after overhearing conversations at work?

Answer: D — Freya Freya says she applied to study law ‘almost entirely because of what I'd overheard’ at the law firm — overhearing conversations led directly to a new career direction.

5. Which person first job directly led to their current profession?

Answer: B — Elena Elena says the bakery job is ‘the reason’ she later trained as a pastry chef — a direct line from that first job to her profession.

6. Which person mentions still being friends with colleagues from that job many years later?

Answer: C — Tomasz Tomasz explicitly says some colleagues from the supermarket job are still close friends over a decade on.

7. Which person started working for a family member?

Answer: B — Elena Elena's first job was in her aunt's bakery — a family member's business.

8. Which person admits they were not good at the tasks the job required?

Answer: A — Marcus Marcus openly admits he ‘was terrible’ at the persuasion the sales job required.

9. Which person describes the work itself as boring, but valued something else about the environment?

Answer: D — Freya Freya calls the receptionist work ‘fairly dull’ but says being around real legal discussions is ‘what hooked me’ — boring task, valuable surroundings.

10. Which person mentions a physical discomfort connected to the job?

Answer: B — Elena Elena mentions brutal early hours and being permanently covered in flour — physical aspects of the job.
