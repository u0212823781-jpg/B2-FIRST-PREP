Part 1 — Multiple-choice Cloze

Exam task: For each gap, choose the correct answer (A, B, C or D).

The rise of remote work

Ten years ago, working from home a few days a week was seen as an unusual perk, available only to a lucky [1]. Today, it has become the norm for millions of office workers, a change that was, in [2], forced on companies almost overnight in 2020. Many businesses that had spent years [3] against remote working suddenly had no choice but to allow it, and discovered, somewhat to their surprise, that productivity didn't [4] as badly as they had feared. Employees, meanwhile, quickly grew used to the extra time they [5] by no longer commuting, and were reluctant to give it [6] once offices reopened. Employers now find themselves under [7] to offer flexible arrangements, or risk losing staff to competitors who will. Whether this shift proves to be permanent or not, it has already changed what employees have come [8] expect from a job.

Gap 1

Answer: few “a lucky few” means a small group of lucky people.

Gap 2

Answer: fact “in fact” is the normal way to add emphasis.

Gap 3

Answer: arguing we say “argue against something”.

Gap 4

Answer: suffer “suffer” fits with “productivity” to mean “get worse”.

Gap 5

Answer: saved “save time” means you used your time well.

Gap 6

Answer: up “give up” means to stop having something.

Gap 7

Answer: pressure “under pressure to do something” is the normal phrase.

Gap 8

Answer: to “come to expect something” means you start expecting it over time.

Part 2 — Open Cloze

Exam task: Think of ONE word only which fits each gap.

Why we forget names

Most people, [1] some point in their lives, have experienced the awkward moment of forgetting someone's name seconds after being introduced to them. Psychologists [2] studied this phenomenon for decades, and their explanation is more reassuring than most of us expect: names are, linguistically speaking, [3] arbitrary label with no real connection to the person themselves. Unlike a job or a hobby, [4] name gives our brain nothing meaningful to attach it [5], which is exactly why it slips away so easily. Interestingly, research [6] shown that simply repeating a name aloud immediately after hearing it can significantly improve [7] well we remember it. So next time you're introduced to someone new, don't just nod politely — say their name [8] loud.

Gap 1.
Answer: at “at some point” means “at a moment we don’t know”.
Gap 2.
Answer: have “have studied” shows the action started before and is still true now.
Gap 3.
Answer: an we use “an” before a word that starts with a vowel sound, like “apple”.
Gap 4.
Answer: a “a” goes before “name”, like “a job” or “a hobby”.
Gap 5.
Answer: to “attach something to something” is the normal phrase.
Gap 6.
Answer: has “research” is one thing, so it goes with “has”.
Gap 7.
Answer: how “improve how well” needs “how” here.
Gap 8.
Answer: out We say “say something out loud” to mean you speak in a loud voice, not just think it.

Part 3 — Word Formation

Exam task: Use the word given in capitals to form a word that fits each gap.

The truth about multitasking

Most of us like to think we're capable of [1] between several tasks at once without any loss of quality, but research paints a far less flattering picture. Neuroscientists have found that what we call multitasking is, in [2], just rapid switching between tasks — and that switching comes at a real [3] cost every single time. Even brief interruptions, studies show, can cause a noticeable drop in [4], sometimes taking several minutes to fully recover from. Perhaps most [5], people who multitask frequently tend to rate their own performance more highly than people who focus on one task at a time — despite performing objectively worse. None of this makes multitasking entirely [6], of course; some low-effort tasks genuinely can be combined without much cost. The [7] advice, researchers suggest, is simple: save true focus for anything that actually requires [8] thought.

Gap 1. SWITCH
Answer: switching after “capable of” we need the “-ing” form. SWITCH → switching.
Gap 2. REAL
Answer: reality “in reality” is the normal phrase. REAL → reality.
Gap 3. PRODUCE
Answer: productivity we need a noun before “cost”. PRODUCE → productivity.
Gap 4. PERFORM
Answer: performance we need a noun after “a drop in”. PERFORM → performance.
Gap 5. SURPRISE
Answer: surprisingly this word describes the whole sentence. SURPRISE → surprisingly.
Gap 6. USE
Answer: useless a negative word after “entirely”. USE → useless.
Gap 7. PRACTICE
Answer: practical a describing word before “advice”. PRACTICE → practical.
Gap 8. CONCENTRATE
Answer: concentrated a describing word before “thought”. CONCENTRATE → concentrated.

Part 4 — Key Word Transformation

Complete the second sentence so that it has a similar meaning to the first sentence, using the word given. Do not change the word given. You must use between two and five words, including the word given.

Mixed grammar review 2

1. It's not necessary for you to finish the report today. NEED
You the report today.
Answer: don't need to finish (You don't need to finish the report today.) We use don’t need to + verb to say something is not necessary.
2. She started working here five years ago. FOR
She here for five years.
Answer: has worked (She has worked here for five years.) We use has + past participle + for to say how long something has continued up to now.
3. 'Don't touch that wire!' the electrician told us. WARNED
The electrician that wire.
Answer: warned us not to touch (The electrician warned us not to touch that wire.) We use warn someone not to + verb to report an order telling someone not to do something.
4. I regret not studying harder for the exam. WISH
I harder for the exam.
Answer: wish I had studied (I wish I had studied harder for the exam.) We use wish + had + past participle to talk about regret about the past.
5. The manager cancelled the meeting because of the storm. CALLED
The meeting because of the storm.
Answer: was called off (The meeting was called off because of the storm.) We use was/were + called off to say something was cancelled.
6. Despite the rain, the match continued. ALTHOUGH
, the match continued.
Answer: Although it was raining (Although it was raining, the match continued.) We use although + subject + verb to contrast two ideas. “Although” needs a full sentence after it, not just a noun like “despite” does.
